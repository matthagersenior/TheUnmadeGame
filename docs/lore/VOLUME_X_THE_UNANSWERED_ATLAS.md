# THE UNMADE — VOLUME X: THE UNANSWERED ATLAS

### A primary storyworld volume, not merely a companion manual

*New literary canon and future-adaptation reference · 10 October 2026 · Preproduction edition*

> "A history is not a place where the dead live. It is a door the living decide to open."

The Unmade is not the story of a chosen heir instructed to defeat a god. It is a universe in which ordinary people have made practical use of impossible omissions. Someone withheld a sound; another society sold the absence as tomorrow's debt; others hung their houses from a missing note, built a wall from an erased lineage, counted citizens who could not have been born, and obeyed a crown that no one accepted. The problem is not that everything is fake. The problem is that everything has become *real to someone*.

This volume supplies a living literary foundation for stories capable of standing without the player protagonist. It fixes recognizable civic worlds, performers' character anchors, motifs, reliable callbacks, and unresolved questions. The storytelling contract is humane and exact: you may create a refuge without erasing an archive; you may reveal exploitation without exposing someone who has chosen privacy. Neither concern becomes an easy morality button.

**Continuity classification:** The names and stable IDs cited here are already present in the game sources. The new physical details, private character motivations, and extended callbacks are **new editorial canon for future writing**, not automatically implemented Unreal scenes, recorded dialogue, or changed save outcomes. A character's theory is not a narrator's fact. An OPEN mystery stays open until a deliberate, separately reviewed lore decision.

## 1. The narrative promise

Every book, episode, film, optional quest, lore codex, or future expansion follows six rules:

1. **Make the place unforgettable.** A viewer who sees Saltwake's skeletal piers beneath pounding rain should know the city before any title card. Its missing sea has civic consequences, not only strange lighting.
2. **Give every human a private life.** Hessa's challenge is not "give quest"; it is that her refuge's secrecy protects people and can also deny them the justice they need. Yllesh's fear is real; so is Orru's refusal.
3. **Remember who knows what.** No character can repeat the player's private choices in another realm without firsthand evidence, public publication, or a delivered account. Delayed or changed couriers can create dramatic consequences, but not magic omniscience.
4. **Answer local mysteries, keep larger ones honest.** Repair the ferry, protect the singers' rest, contest a debt. The sealed chart's author, the unoccupied lectern's handprint and the second impossible footsteps may remain unanswered.
5. **Let callbacks change meaning.** The refuge cup begins as a kindness, returns as documentary proof of a hidden household, and becomes an image of voluntary remembrance. Merely hiding the same symbol in backgrounds is not storytelling.
6. **Protect the player-created protagonist.** The novels and screen stories can use Hessa, Sella, Yllesh or Issil as protagonists. They cannot decree a single canon player sex, race, heritage, morality or mutually exclusive postboss future.

### The signature grammar

**The Unanswered Interval** is usually not audible as silence. It is what becomes *conspicuously missing amid normal life*: the absent end of a bell note under the clatter of cups; no wave-slap behind rain; a furnace burn without fuel; seven rests recorded in a six-part choir. Never cut every ambient sound merely to tell an audience they should feel uneasy. Let a character's hand or eye betray what they expected to hear.

**Social objects are keys to memory**, not meaningless collectibles: the uncounted cup, dry rope knot, cooled coal, backward ledger, broken tuning fork, protected name-rubbing, voluntarily tied doorway cord, untouched orchard fruit, and departure footprint.

**An answer costs something.** It can cost a reputation, a livelihood, a public promise or the comfort of an institution. It should never require nonconsensual exposure as an unchallenged narrative reward.

## 2. A city must be instantly identifiable

These eleven settlements inherit their exact canonical names and realm assignments from the living-world atlas. Their following shapes, sounds, civic disputes and repeatable images are now the adaptation keys. A local episode has to begin in *that* city rather than interchangeable medieval streets.

### 1. The Crossings — three roads meet under one missing mile

**Silhouette:** Timber footbridges tied with three competing knots; street tiles from three abandoned maps. The unmistakable emblem is **three mismatched road knots**.

**What an audience hears:** A humming roadstone counterpoint to market kettles. **How people behave:** Wards refuse to ask a traveler for a name until they have eaten.

**Power and friction:** Bridge Ledger Court. Orrel marks crossings with an inkless nail; Kesta can deliver testimony but cannot decide which road is right

**The unsettled incident:** The third road runs through an address erased from Paperhaven's publicly dated surveys.

**Adaptation guard:** Keep the city's material signature, audible identity, civic disagreement, and visible ordinary life; never make its paradox a generic fantasy portal.

## 3. The people who can carry a novel or television season

A camera should recognize a returning Unmade character by silhouette, gesture, habit, and the decision they cannot easily make—not by title cards or mystical exposition. These thirteen leading identities are drawn from the 82 already registered residents. Their original stable actor IDs are unchanged. No new character here replaces an established person.

### 1. Hessa the refuge matron

*Persistent ID: `npc.bellwold.matron.001`*

**Recognizable before a name is spoken:** One thumb permanently marked by hot kettle handles; refuses to stand behind the registration desk. Their recurring prop is **an uncounted porcelain cup, chipped on its lip**.

**Public responsibility:** Keep the refuge open through a second night of danger. **Private hunger:** Be believed without having to surrender the protected names of the people she saved. **Human failing:** Quietly decides what others are permitted to know, even when the secrecy becomes paternalism.

**The old wound:** A child she sheltered was once made into a census entry against her promise. **Voice and performance:** Warm contralto; ends arguments by asking whether someone has eaten.

**What they tell themselves:** “If nobody writes down a name, nobody can use it to hurt them.” **What their story tests:** Her mercy can conceal harms that records might prevent.

**Two living bonds:** Sorin wants triage first; she asks him to make room for one more traveler (`npc.bellwold.healer.001`); Ivera asks her to admit when protection becomes silence (`npc.bellwold.childtutor.001`); Sevrin distrusts unverified refuge numbers (`npc.paperhaven.registrar.001`).

**A first line:** “We keep one cup uncounted. It is not missing.” **When we return:** After a later return, she carries the same cup but lets its owner decide where to sit.

**Question that stays:** Why does the cup's hairline crack echo the uncast bell's seam?

### 2. Ivera the tutor

*Persistent ID: `npc.bellwold.childtutor.001`*

**Recognizable before a name is spoken:** Chalk dust up to one wrist; looks at people's shoes when adults discuss who belongs. Their recurring prop is **a lesson slate scored with seven lines but only six numerals**.

**Public responsibility:** Teach the displaced children to read public records critically. **Private hunger:** Protect pupils without teaching them that facts are optional. **Human failing:** Assumes disclosure is always kinder if written clearly.

**The old wound:** An absent name in a class register belonged to a child she knew. **Voice and performance:** Fast precise phrasing that slows around protected testimony.

**What they tell themselves:** “Any true entry ought to be public.” **What their story tests:** A right to truth can threaten a right to remain anonymous.

**Two living bonds:** Hessa once hid the very document she needs to teach with (`npc.bellwold.matron.001`); Sorin witnesses the consequences of Ivera's certainty (`npc.bellwold.healer.001`).

**A first line:** “If the number is wrong, why is the child still here?” **When we return:** On return she teaches the same lesson with a blank line deliberately left intact.

**Question that stays:** The seventh slate line appears on stones she has never visited.

### 3. Sevrin the registrar

## 4. Original literary scene: The Uncounted Cup

*Bellwold. A first-volume opening scene. This is narrative source, not an in-engine cutscene.*

The bell failed to ring while Hessa was pouring the last cup.

It was not the silence that troubled her. At this hour the refuge made every ordinary sound of survival: pots catching each other in a sink, the dragging cough of old Corvin returning from the masonry yard, a child asking whether tomorrow had a proper name. Beyond the shelter walls, the tower kept its empty place above the roofs. Those who had known Bellwold longer than its official records said a bell had once hung there. The records said no metal had ever been cast.

Hessa did not care what the records said before breakfast.

She counted eight bowls on the table. There were seven people seated. Then she filled the ninth cup, the chipped white one she never included in the tally, and left it by the door.

Ivera watched from the steps, slate beneath her arm. She had chalk on her wrist and the angry patience of a teacher who knew that an explanation was being withheld.

"Eight bowls," Ivera said.

"Seven eaters."

"Nine cups."

"One is for someone who may come."

"Or someone who did?"

Hessa turned the kettle so the hot handle faced away from the little girl nearest the fire. It was a gesture she made without thinking, years after anyone had asked her to. "Both people are welcome," she said.

Ivera did not write that down. Instead she set the slate on the table, beside the cup. Across its surface ran seven tidy horizontal lines. Only six of them carried numbers.

"This line appeared yesterday," she said.

"Chalk does not appear."

"Children are remarkably good at chalk, yes. But this was below a sum I did not let them change. If they are lying, they're very clever. If they aren't—"

"Perhaps you should ask what the seventh line means to them."

"I did. They said it was where the bell went."

At that moment the watchman opened the door. A traveler stood behind him with a child's cloak bundled against one shoulder. Rain had touched the traveler's shoes but not the cloak. Their face was the face of anyone who had walked farther than they intended and could not be sure the road behind them would still be there by afternoon.

"Have you a place?" the watchman asked.

Hessa lifted the uncounted cup. "We've a chair."

"I don't want to be entered," said the traveler.

Ivera put a hand on her slate.

"No one asked for your name," Hessa answered.

"You will eventually."

"I might. You needn't give it."

The traveler looked toward the bell tower. In the space where its last note should have hung, the market outside continued calling prices. A child laughed. A horse knocked its shoe against a cobble. For an interval no longer than a held breath, the traveler appeared to know a place no one had named.

The cloak shifted. Under it the bundled shape was only a rolled blanket.

Hessa saw Ivera see it too.

For the first time that morning, neither woman knew whether kindness or evidence ought to speak first.

The traveler took the cup. There was a fine crack in the porcelain, running from the chipped lip toward the base. Ivera would later insist she had seen that same shape in the official rubbing of a bell that had never been cast. Hessa would remember only the traveler's thumb resting across it, as if the cup might open.

When the traveler asked where to put the blanket, Hessa showed them an empty bed.

By noon, the bed was warm.

By night, the guest was gone. Nobody had heard the door.

The cup remained. It was the last thing Hessa washed, and the first thing she set out again.

**Knowledge ledger:** Hessa witnessed the traveler and the empty blanket. Ivera saw the crack and believed it matched a rubbing; she has not proved that connection. No page confirms the traveler's identity or turns the scene into a hidden royal-origin prophecy. Both characters remain ethically legible.

## 5. Original literary scene: The Seventh Rest

*Orravane. A later stand-alone story opening, several generations of choral engineering into the city's inhabited history.*

The day Orravane nearly fell, nobody screamed.

The singers did what they had been trained to do since childhood. They widened their stance, touched the rail with two fingers, and gave the supporting note as a single note should be given: steady, without the small vanity of ornament, with enough breath behind it to keep the hanging street from shifting. Beneath them, the lower houses waited in the wrong direction. Soil sprinkled upward from a garden bed.

There were six throats at the rail. Yllesh heard seven voices.

He knew what every one of those singers could bear. He kept the chart himself: morning pitches, evening recoveries, the faint tremor that entered an exhausted voice three breaths before the singer admitted they needed rest. The seventh sound belonged to no name on his chart. It had come before, sometimes when all six held their pitch and sometimes during an authorized pause. He had stopped recording it years ago.

Orru stood at the end of the rail, arms crossed. The empty seventh lectern was between them.

"Dismiss us," Orru said.

"The east houses are still moving."

"We can hear them. They always move. Dismiss us."

Yllesh followed the sound into the joints above the lower stair. Somewhere inside the timber a load was traveling without a beam to carry it. A hundred people lived beneath that stair. Orru knew it. So did every singer whose mouth had gone pale from holding a note.

"If we stop now—"

"That is what you always say before we stop."

"Look at the marks on the rail."

"I put those marks there."

Yllesh knew. Orru had scratched one line for each day a promised rest had been postponed. The scratches ran almost the entire length of the rail. Every time he reached their beginning he told himself the city had survived because he had been strong enough to be cruel.

"One more phrase," he said.

Orru did not sing.

The other five kept their tone. One by one their mouths began to shake, not from terror but from exhaustion held too long for pride. A roof shifted. The floor pressed lightly upward against Yllesh's boots, as though the world were trying to stand where he stood.

Then Orru placed a hand upon the seventh lectern.

The missing note stopped.

A support bead slid along its wire and came to rest in the vacant socket. The east houses did not fall. They settled a hand's breadth lower and stayed there.

Yllesh waited for the scream, the crack, the unmistakable sound of the city proving him right.

Instead he heard one singer breathe. Then another. A window opened below them and someone shouted up to ask why it had suddenly become possible to hear the rain.

"Rest," Orru told the others.

Only when they had gone did Yllesh inspect the lectern. Beneath the polished rim, where there should have been no human wear, a shallow groove had been made by a hand resting in the same place for a long time. The wood was warm.

He kept both discoveries to himself until dawn. He told the engineers about the bead. He had always been able to speak of weight.

He did not tell them about the hand.

**Knowledge ledger:** Yllesh witnessed the load settling and later examined a worn lectern; Orru felt the mechanism respond. Neither knows whether a person, a memory, or a pressure phenomenon produced it. This mystery does not absolve Yllesh of earlier coercion or require singers to become magical fuel.

## 10. A film need not tell the same story as the series

A self-contained film can follow **The Seventh Rest**: a choral-engineering disaster story with Orru, Yllesh and the city's tired families; worldbuilding is revealed by labor, architecture, consent and a missing sound. A sequel or anthology can later revisit the worn lectern as a historical object without exposing the central unsolved phenomenon in the first film. The film ends after a real civic accomplishment: the residents establish rest without letting the town collapse.

A novel can instead center **The Cup No Census Claimed**, a moral mystery about hiding a person's identity and preserving the documents necessary to protect them. Its ending does not certify one universal backstory for the player hero.

## 11. The rules for expansions, cameos and adaptations

1. Characters may have medium-specific dialogue but their personal motives, name, origin region and limits on knowledge remain consistent.
2. No series/novel/movie declares one mutually exclusive player ending the universal canonical ending; adapt with a visible variant label or set before the divergence.
3. A local humane victory must not claim that a cosmic villain personally perpetrated every historical injustice.
4. A mystery may yield a satisfying local answer while its larger pattern remains legitimately unanswered.
5. Distinctive icons, sound motifs, materials and gestures recur naturally; no arbitrary Easter eggs without a provenance path.
6. The protagonist's appearance, ancestry and chosen ethics remain free; side protagonists can carry standalone screen stories.

**Canon and evidence hierarchy.** An original game-state save, quest gate or fixed actor ID cannot be overturned by a prose interpretation. A source-observed event outranks a rumor. A writer may depict honest incompatible witnesses; they cannot invent an answer by declaring one witness unknowingly omniscient. The two post-Nhal-Vey futures are both legitimate variants, not 'right' and 'fake'.

**Mystery release discipline.** OPEN mysteries remain open in scripts, promo copy, AI dialogue, branching side content and supplementary fiction. To resolve one requires a separate reviewed canon decision: what evidence was already present, which people learn it, what existing events change, how future media callbacks work, and which parts remain unknowable.

**Production truth.** This is *new editorial primary-source storyworld material* attached to stable source identifiers. It is not a UE-compiled game, a completed professionally voiced film, or a promise that every scene here has collision, animation or dialog code. Future assets are traceable by city, character, mystery and callback IDs.

## 17. The Witness Braid — An Inquiry That Refuses a Crown
*Volume X playable interlude · October 10, 2026 · new source-implemented optional storyline*

The Crossings had three roads and three surveyors. Orrel used to say that when all three disagreed, they had at least proved there was ground beneath their feet. Then a blue-corded nail began trembling whenever a traveler said they had come from a place the courts no longer acknowledged.

**The Witness Braid** is what Orrel calls the work of setting incompatible firsthand objects side by side without taking possession of their people. Its principal antagonists are not monsters. They are the ordinary pressure to make a record convenient for the institution reading it, and the equally dangerous temptation to hide a record that could protect someone else.

### Episode scene: three cities, one interval

At **Bellwold**, Hessa's uncounted refuge cup is chipped on one side. She is not sure when it broke. She refuses to have her guests treated as the evidence needed to date it. The public Afterlight choice may make her cup a sign of shelter or documentary testimony, but does not reveal a missing bell-maker.

At **Paperhaven**, Sevrin keeps the reverse of a seal press blank. The Index Hall can add an annotation to a record without publishing a sleeping guest's name. The press bears a date that, according to the most careful archivists, should precede the press's own use. The player is not entitled to conclude that the guest is the impossible person.

At **the Crossings**, Orrel's inkless nail marks a third road, yet the official surveys differ about whether that road ever had a place in the city. Kesta may eventually carry an account to another neighborhood, but only as testimony she could physically deliver. She is not an omniscient courier.

Nothing about these three objects establishes a cosmic verdict. Each is a separate, locally witnessed trace. **Only after personally inspecting all three and returning to at least one after a legitimate local story outcome has changed its meaning** can the player make a public response at Orrel's nail. No book, prophecy, paid upgrade or guardian boss bypasses those observations.

### A choice with architecture, not a cosmetic dialogue reward

The same familiar F7 and F8 controls used by other irreversible decisions require two matching presses inside the game's six-second commitment gate. The player must be physically beside the inkless nail. If a final local quest witness takes priority, that earlier obligation is resolved through its original flow; the Braid never steals another quest's choice.

**F7 — The Sheltered Thread.** A low, horizontal refuge cord becomes physically present nearby. It guides unnamed travelers toward an established shelter without claiming who owns their histories. That protection comes at a cost: Sevrin cannot easily present the complete pattern in the public archive. Future writing can revisit someone excluded from a hearing because the lead remained private. The source currently shows the structure and journals the result; it does not yet implement those later conversations or city-wide AI navigation.

**F8 — The Public Docket.** A taller public record appears instead. It contains the *objects' contradictions* but no protected individuals' names. A witness can challenge an institution with this evidence, yet hostile readers might begin hunting the people who were never named. Orrel fears what publishing even a redacted question could invite. The source currently shows the record, writes the choice and reports a local player event; it does not simulate distant reprisals that have not yet been authored.

Both remain valid, mutually exclusive save outcomes. Neither produces a magical reward, forces an ending or tells the player they chose evil. The named NPCs continue to have private lives outside the protagonist's decision.

### Why the mystery remains alive

There are at least three incompatible causal hypotheses, each belonging to a **speaker**, not to an authoritative omniscient narrator:

- **Hessa's account:** the cup was chipped by a guest who has been erased from the count. This would make refuge an intervention in history, but she cannot prove when it happened.
- **Sevrin's account:** the inked side of the seal was made during a routine records correction, while the undated side is a later form of intentional consent. This would make the missing entry a policy choice rather than a temporal rupture.
- **Orrel's account:** a road that was taxed before it was built exposed the contradiction, and the records adapted afterward. That suggests the map failed first, but his nail cannot provide a date.

No automatically awarded quest text chooses among these hypotheses. They are prompts for later revisits, dialogue, an anthology episode, and optional future expansions. The broader nine-realm unresolved questions, customizable protagonist origin, Nhal-Vey's false victory and both alternate mornings are unchanged.

## 18. The Living Evidence — The Second Truth Is That Someone Told You
*Volume X playable / adaptation continuity chapter · 10 October 2026*

The phenomenon that most unsettles Sevrin is not the archive's impossible date. It is a perfectly ordinary sentence spoken by a neighbor: **“I know because someone told me.”** The neighbor may be sincere, mistaken, courageous, afraid, or protecting somebody. The sentence is evidence of a conversation, not evidence that the conversation's account was true.

### 18.1 Three objects; three minds

**Hessa / CALL.01 — The Cup.** A refugee under her protection may have chipped the cup before being removed from public records. Hessa *suspects* this and has excellent reasons to distrust any clerk eager to publish the theory. Her private cost: protecting unnamed guests can also leave an exploited household with no paper proof of its existence. Never make the player discover a protected guest's identity as a free collectible reward.

**Sevrin / CALL.03 — The Reverse Seal.** Sevrin believes the unmarked face records a deliberate consent limit. He can demonstrate how a record can be amended without naming its vulnerable subject. But its contradictory date might precede the seal press's own manufacture. His private cost: delaying a correction can become a form of institutional self-protection; he does not always notice when he crosses that line.

**Orrel / CALL.04 — The Inkless Nail.** Orrel suspects the disputed third road was taxed before its route was agreed. He remembers carrying wood beside surveyors who disagreed over where the road lay. Three surveys can all be physically present and still contradict one another. His private cost: he owes wages to builders the city denies it employed.

A player who reads Hessa's cup does **not** automatically learn Sevrin's account. An accessible notebook has slots for only *firsthand physically inspected* relics. On returning after a legitimate local civic decision, the same object may be witnessed again under a different interpretation; that is a change in the available account, not a retroactive rewrite of the first memory.

### 18.2 The map of knowledge is not the map of the world

The evidence notebook is a record **of how the player came to know**, not a canonical verdict tree. It may show the author's claim and the best known objection. Claims must remain attributed to Hessa, Sevrin and Orrel, not to “THE UNIVERSE.” The objective facts are the physical objects, the player's authenticated inspections, saved civic outcomes and the private publication decision. All deeper causal explanations remain OPEN.

A found object has three stages:

1. **Hidden:** Until the player physically visits and inspects the scene, the object's identity, theory and location-specific objection cannot appear in their notebook.
2. **Personally observed:** The initial text, named author and challenge become visible; neither an NPC's unexplained guess nor a stale memory substitutes for a visit.
3. **Observed again:** After a valid local choice changes its public meaning, a return interaction preserves both the earlier state and the new reading. This is not proof that one was a lie.

The full cross-city comparison is unlocked only by three personal observations and one changed revisit, regardless of the order in which the player visits. An inaccessible city does not automatically grant clues. A future localization must preserve the explicit markers for *speaker, hearsay and firsthand evidence*.

### 18.3 The people do not have global quest-state vision

When the player publishes the redacted docket, an NPC who physically sees it go up can say:

> “I saw the redacted docket posted. It names no guest; I worry who will search for the people it does not name.”

That resident may become concerned even if the player's intention was benign. If a second resident hears that account from the first in person, they may say:

> “Someone told me a redacted docket was posted. I have not seen it and cannot confirm their explanation.”

They must name their source rather than pretending they stood at the Crossings. The second statement is not an eyewitness account, cannot be propagated again as one, and cannot be used to make a final quest judgment. The same distinction governs the Sheltered Thread's physical refuge cord.

**Rules of knowledge propagation:** An on-site act emits a *distinct* source event for sheltered versus public response; real local line of sight determines which residents directly observe it. An existing spatial conversation loop passes an eyewitness account to a nearby listener as **rumor** with a stable speaker ID. No absent resident, unseen distant city, generic AI output or global story flag confers the event to every NPC. Player dialogue, trust and fear may respond to the event **only if that resident has it in personal memory**.

**Limitations of today's implementation:** The graybox supports a read-only F9 notebook and Blueprint-returned text, source-limited individual dialogue and local one-hop memory propagation.
